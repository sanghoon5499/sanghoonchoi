Chapter I: Setting the Stage

Protein is one of the most important nutrients that we need to survive. They are the building blocks of life; every cell in the human body contains protein. We need it to repair cells and make new ones, so whether you’re a child, a pregnant woman, or a seasoned body builder, it’s important that protein is part of your daily intake of nutrients.

Proteins are polymers, meaning they are made of a chain of structural units. These units make up one protein molecule. When we consume protein, our bodies break it down into the individual amino acids. Amino acids are found in animal sources like meats, milk, fish, and eggs. They are also found in plant sources like beans, soy, legumes, and some grains.

Amino acids are classified into three groups:

  • Essential: Cannot be produced by the body - we need to eat food to get them
  • Nonessential: Can be produced by the body
  • Conditional: Needed when we’re ill or stressed

While amino acids have a large variety of roles that extend beyond building protein, I only want to cover amino acids and protein in this article. Even though this is a narrow scope of amino acids, there’s a lot of interesting things to cover.

Chapter II: Evolution and the Amino Acids in Protein


II Part 1: The Primordial Soup

To get to the protein we know today, we need to travel back four billion years ago. Just 500 million years into Earth’s chaotic history, the planet was a mixture of chemicals waiting for something to happen. This is the era of the “primordial soup”. There are a lot of theories out there on how the first ingredients for life appeared on Earth. Some argue that they arrived on meteorites or bubbled up from deep-sea hydrothermal vents. I found one narrative particularly compelling, and that one begins with the ocean.

Primordial soup gets its name from being a soup of chemicals before life ever existed. Water, methane, and ammonia underwent chemical reactions under the sun’s harsh UV rays (Earth had no protective barrier yet - the ozone layer) and lightning strikes. The results of these reactions were simple sugars, fatty acids, and amino acids. But having bricks is not the same as having a house.

What house you may ask? Well RNA of course. How these molecules formed RNA is a great mystery even today. In the open ocean our building blocks would drift apart. Sugar decomposes in water, and genetic bases don’t bond very well either. How then, was RNA able to form?

The solution lay not in the vastness of the ocean, but on the edges of volcanoes. In 2009, John Sutherland, a chemist at the University of Manchester, proposed that maybe life actually assembled itself in geothermal pools like the ones at Yellowstone today.

Geothermal pools often went through a wet-dry cycle. During the wet phase, the chemicals mixed and reacted, forming ribonucleotides. Under the scorching sun, the water dried up and the formed ribonucleotides were pulled close and chained together. In the drying mud of a volcanic spring, the long chains of ribonucleotides were given a name: RNA.

But these pools were dangerous to early RNA - filled with sulfur and constantly given a good dose of acid rain, the RNA needed a container. A good solution was found in the fatty acids which provided a bubble to contain itself in. Unfortunately, the RNA soon found itself with another problem: the fatty molecules were weak to pH changes. Through evolution, phospholipids eventually became strong enough armor for RNA. When the pools began to overflow, these armored cells washed downstream into the salty ocean. They survived the salt that should’ve destroyed them.

Over eons, these survivors underwent significant change. DNA was evolved to store genetic blueprints and proteins took charge of reading them to build the cell. This evolution transformed these simple RNA bubbles to the first true living cells.

In the ocean, life began to diversify. A major split occurred when these simple cells decided to form into bacteria and archaea. The bacteria developed a protein called MreB to maintain shape. Archaea (our ancestors) formed its own version called crenactin.

Something amazing happened sometime between 1.6 to 2.2 billion years ago. An archaean cell swallowed a bacterium, but failed to digest it. This swallowed bacteria eventually became the mitochondria that we know today. As the need for structure arose, the bacteria’s MreB was repurposed into actin. To move around, a protein called myosin was evolved. This multi-cellular organism, complete with a nucleus, spare energy, and actin-myosin proteins, was LECA (the Last Eukaryotic Common Ancestor).
From this point on, every animal that has ever swam, crawled, or walked, from the trilobite to the human, is a direct descendant of this one freak accident.

II Part 2: How Animals Move

When actin and myosin were invented in the first eukaryotic cell (LECA), they were built from a specific sequence of amino acids. This sequence was more than a list; it was a blueprint for a 3D shape. In the molecular world, shape defines function. By changing even one amino acid, the protein fails to do its job (a job called “protein folding”) and the cell dies.

Evolution settled on a specific sequence of 20 amino acids that produced the most efficient way to convert chemical energy into motion. Because this was so efficient, it has remained almost exactly the same over 2 billion years. We call these “highly conserved proteins”. Today, from sea sponges to humans, the recipe remains virtually identical because it’s the only recipe that works. While the proteins didn’t change, the way they are arranged sure did.

  • Early days: Single-celled eukaryotes had actin and myosin scattered randomly. This allowed the cells to squish and stretch, allowing for an early form of movement.
  • Smooth muscles: Evolution eventually arranged these proteins into a lattice or web. Like a drawstring bag, cells contracted inward from all sides. This was perfect for slow, sustained squeezing, which is why we find these muscles in our intestines or the trapping muscles of a clam.
  • Striated muscles: To swim, run, or fly, animals needed directional power. Evolution organized the proteins into parallel rows called sarcomeres. With millions of units pulling in unison, these muscles became exceptional for endurance and rapid, forceful actions.

II Part 3: Outsourcing

Every animal has actin and myosin, and since they are highly conserved proteins, every animal shares the same recipe for them too. A cow’s muscle is built from almost the exact same amino acid sequence as a human’s muscle. If 15% of the amino acids in a cow’s muscle are leucine, then roughly 15% of a human’s muscle will be leucine too.

In order to survive, the necessary 20 amino acids originally had to be created within the body. But synthesizing these complex molecules requires massive amounts of energy. As animals began eating other organisms, evolution asked the golden question: why build when you can steal?

Our early ancestors were already consuming protein-rich foods - which we saw earlier, were already packed with the exact amino acids we needed. Over millions of years, animals began to shut down the factories for the amino acids that were the hardest to make. We lost the ability to make 9 of them. These became known as the “essential amino acids”, not because they are more important, but because it is essential that we eat them.

There is a catch-22 here: if we can’t make these 9 amino acids, and the cow we eat can’t make them either, where are they coming from? We find the answer by diving into the food chain:

  1. Plants: Grass contains all 20 amino acids, but often in small, scattered amounts. Plants must make them all because they cannot eat. They forge these molecules using nitrogen from the soil, carbon from the air, and energy from the sun.
  2. Herbivores: The cow is like a filter. It spends its entire life eating a whole lot of grass, absorbing the amino acids during digestion.
  3. Carnivores: When we eat a juicy steak, we are eating the life's work of that cow. It has conveniently converted millions of blades of grass into a neat, dense block of muscle.

This extends beyond just cows to every animal in the food chain. Chickens eat grains and insects (which ate plants); fish eat smaller fish which ate algae. Ultimately, there is no such thing as “animal protein” - all protein is originally plant protein which has just been repackaged. Carnivorous plants like venus flytraps are a fascinating exception. They evolved to eat insects specifically because they live in nitrogen-poor soil, forcing them to “steal” amino acids just like animals do.

Chapter III: The Limiting Amino Acid

We saw earlier that 9 amino acids are essential to us. These amino acids are: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, threonine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, and valine. Each amino acid plays a slightly different role in our bodies than simply making the protein chain. However in this section, I want to specifically cover the limiting amino acid.

Imagine you own a car production plant. To make a car, you need a steering wheel, an engine, 4 seats, and 4 tires. Now imagine you had 1000 steering wheels, 1000 engines, 4000 seats, and only 4 tires. Despite the massive number of steering wheels, engines, and seats, the 4 tires dictate how many cars will be produced by your factory: just one.

This is no different when it comes to protein production. Protein is the car, and the amino acids are the car parts. You need a certain amount of them each to synthesize one protein. This is important when it comes to understanding why some proteins are “complete” and why others are “incomplete”.

  • Complete: All animal proteins are complete - they contain all 9 essential amino acids in the right ratios.
  • Incomplete: Most plant proteins are incomplete - they are low in, or lack, one or more of the 9 essential amino acids.

For example, let’s take lentils. Highly nutritious, and an affordable plant-based protein. Boasting around 15 grams of protein per half cup, they seem perfect, right? Looking at their PDCAAS score (Protein Digestibility Corrected Amino Acid Score) though, they hover around 0.6. This translates to 15 grams × 0.6 = 9 grams of usable protein. The limited amount of methionine and cysteine prevent your body from using the other amino acids present.

This isn’t to say avoid lentils. If you can manage to eat food that has methionine (like rice and wheat), you can raise the PDCAAS score of the entire meal to a perfect 1.00. You don’t have to look far - seeds/grains + legumes = complete protein. Soy-based foods like tofu are a rare exception; they have a PDCAAS score of 1.0 on their own. But for everything else, pairing your plant proteins gets you most of the way there.

In the last century, we stopped relying on simple food combinations and started isolating the chemistry itself. We have entered the era of protein powders and synthetic supplementation - our modern miracles.

Chapter IV: Modern Miracles

Over 7000 years ago, our ancestors were having a grand old time storing and transporting milk in bags made from animal stomachs. These bladders contained enzymes called rennet, which would encourage curdling. While they picked out the floating chunks (the cheese), the liquids were often dumped as nothing more than a byproduct. Fast forwarding to 400 BC Greece, Hippocrates, who is considered one of the most outstanding figures in the history of medicine, prescribed this waste water to his patients as a health tonic. This waste water was eventually given a name: whey.

It wasn’t until the mid 20th century when scientists learned about amino acids, complete proteins, and how whey is jam-packed of them, that work began to separate these amino acids from them. The result was a fine white powder - almost entirely pure amino acids. Nearly all of the protein powders you see today are created by extracting, isolating, and concentrating amino acids from various food sources.

Chapter VI: Timing Your Protein

Knowing what proteins to take is only half the battle. The other half is knowing when to take them. For this, we turn to Muscle Protein Synthesis (MPS) - the metabolic process where your body actually takes those amino acids and turns them into new muscle tissue.

The Biological Timeline

To understand why timing matters, let’s take a look at what happens when you eat 30g of protein for lunch.

Time Description
12:00 You eat protein. As digestion releases amino acids into the blood, the change in amino acid levels activates MPS.
1:30 MPS is maximized. You are building muscle at the fastest rate possible.
3:00 MPS drops to baseline. This is the refractory period. Even if amino acids are floating in your blood, the muscles are now “full” and stop building.
3:00 to 5:00 To restart the factory, your amino acid levels must drop back to "low." The body’s sensors detect the spike, not the amount. You cannot create a new spike until you have created a valley.

The Refractory Period

The refractory period of MPS kills the idea of the “one giant meal”. If you eat all 100g of protein for dinner, you flood your body for one 4-hour window, leaving MPS idle for the other 20 hours.

This often confuses people because many sources state that there is “no limit” to how much protein you can digest. They are right - your stomach can absorb all 100g, but your muscles can only use about 30g per MPS cycle.

What happens to the other 70g? Since the doors to the muscle are locked and the body has no storage tank for amino acids, the liver takes over. Stripping the nitrogen from the amino acids and converting the rest into glucose and ketones, the liver has created some very “expensive” energy. You just took high-quality protein and converted it to sugar. By the time MPS is ready to start up again, the 70g of excess amino acids are now already gone - burned off or turned into urine.

Triggering MPS

You can’t go to the opposite extreme and consume tiny amounts of protein all day. The factory has a startup cost. To trigger MPS, you need a sudden flood of leucine (~2-3g) in the blood to flip the switch. If you snack on 5g of protein here and there, you’ll never hit the threshold.

Balance is Key

Finally, remember the car factory analogy? We know that you need the engines, seats, tires and steering wheels to build a car. These parts need to arrive at the factory at roughly the same time. If you had a grain-heavy breakfast (limiting amino acid here is lysine), but wait until dinner to supplement the lysine, the amino acids from breakfast will have already been digested as glucose and ketones.

Chapter VII: Is Protein Powder Bad for You?

At this point, you understand the biological reality: your body can’t tell the difference between a leucine molecule from a steak, and a leucine molecule from protein powder. To your cells, they are the same thing.

But the tub of protein powder contains more than just protein; there’s a whole chemistry set on the ingredient label. To really understand if protein powder is bad for you, we have to look at what else is in that scoop.

1. Heavy Metals

Reports that find lead in protein powder often rely on “right to know” warning thresholds that are much lower than actual medical safety limits. In reality, the tiny amount of lead in most protein powder is similar to what you would find in a bowl of spinach or other root vegetables.

Detailed Research

One of the most persistent fears about protein powder is that it’s contaminated with heavy metals like lead, arsenic, and cadmium. This fear largely stems from a viral report by Consumer Reports (CR), a non-profit organization known for product testing.

According to their research, almost all protein powders contained detectable levels of lead, and many exceeded CR’s “safety limit” by upwards of 1500%. CR’s conclusion was stark: avoid protein powder or limit it to a few times a week. Their level of concern for lead is 0.5 micrograms per day. Where is this coming from? Is 0.5 a lot or a little? The lack of info here makes me skeptical on the accuracy of info.

I want to make my stance clear: I’m not advocating that we consume lead-heavy protein powders, down shots of lead with our best friends, and add lead supplements to our favourite dishes. No amount of lead should enter our bodies, but that’s often a tall order. To understand if you should be worried, we need to look at the numbers CR used.

The Warning vs The Limit

CR based their “level of concern” on California’s Proposition 65 standard.

  • What is Prop 65? It’s a “right to know” law, not a medical safety limit. It requires a warning label if a product contains more than 0.5 micrograms (µg) of lead per day.
  • Why 0.5? This limit is set extremely low - 1000 times lower than the “No Observable Effect Level” in animals and humans. This is to force companies to reduce contaminants, not as a line where toxicity begins.

Federal Safety Limits

In contrast, agencies like Health Canada and the FDA set limits based on where actual health risks begin for humans.

  • Health Canada’s limit: 10 µg per day.
  • What this means: A protein powder could have 5 µg of lead and be considered safe by federal health standards, but be flagged as dangerous by CR because it exceeds the 0.5 µg warning threshold.

How Much Lead is There in Protein Powder?

When you look at the data from the CR study, the numbers tell a less scary story:

  • The Worst Offenders: Plant-based proteins tested the highest, with some containing 6.6 to 7.3 µg per serving. Plants absorb heavy metals directly from the soil and water.
  • On Average: A vast majority of protein powders tested contained significantly less. On average, all tested protein powders had lead contents of 1.34 µg. For 19 of the 23 protein powders, the average was only 0.6 µg.

Even the worst plant proteins were still below the federal safety guideline of 10 µg.

How Much Lead is a Lot?

Ideally, zero lead would enter our bodies. But on a planet made of dirt and water, that is impossible. Lead is a naturally occurring element in the Earth's crust, which means it ends up in the water, the soil, and the food grown in that soil.

According to Health Canada, the average person already consumes 0.1 µg per kg of body weight every day just from regular food. If you weigh 60kg, that’s already ~6 µg of lead per day from your salad, water, and grains. A protein shake containing 0.6 µg to 1.3 µg is a drop in the bucket compared to what you are already eating. It might as well be background noise.

It should also be noted that lead poisoning levels have been decreasing every year. This is a good indication that the average daily lead consumption has little impact on most people’s health.

2. Bloating

If you’re East Asian, African, or Native American, there’s a good chance you’re lactose intolerant. The bloating isn’t caused by the protein, it’s caused by the lactose. If you find yourself with stomach problems after drinking protein, try whey isolate or plant-based protein.

  • Whey Concentrate (WPC): Cheaper, less filtered. It retains some fat and lactose.
  • Whey Isolate (WPI): Highly filtered. The fat and lactose are filtered away, leaving 90%+ pure protein.

3. Too Much Protein at Once?

There is a fear that too much protein can destroy your kidneys. It’s true that excessive and extreme protein intake can overwork the liver in people with pre-existing conditions, but high intake is generally safe for healthy individuals.

We learned in an earlier chapter that your muscles can only take in around 30-40g of protein per sitting. If you consume more than this, it’s not your kidneys that will hurt, but your wallet.

Chapter VIII: The Grand Design

We began this journey 4 billion years ago in a bubbling, chaotic pool of chemicals. From that primordial soup, life stumbled upon a molecular design so perfect - actin and myosin - that it hasn’t changed since.

Through the eons, we evolved from single cells squirming in the mud, to complex machines capable of sprinting, lifting, and thinking. To ease the load on these machines, we made a crucial evolutionary decision: stop building every amino acid ourselves and steal the essential ones from the world around us.

Today, modern medicine sets the bar for protein intake too low. For the average adult on a 2000 calorie diet, protein should account for 10 - 35% of daily energy. This equates to roughly 50 - 175 grams of protein.

However, the official Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) is just 0.8 grams per kilogram. If you weigh 65kg (143lbs), the RDA suggests you only need about 52 grams of protein per day. That is the floor required to prevent malnutrition, not the ceiling for optimal health.

When we look at the long game, pushing that intake higher (closer to 1.6 - 2.2 g/kg) unlocks health benefits that go far beyond big biceps.

Sarcopenia Once you hit age 40, sarcopenia (natural muscle loss) begins to set in. To prevent this and to maintain quality of life as you age, your protein intake needs to increase to 1.0 - 1.2 g/kg. If you’re active, that number should jump to 1.1 - 1.5 g/kg just to maintain what you have.
Bone Density Protein increases calcium absorption and strengthens bone structure.
Metabolic Health Protein is the most satiating nutrient and has the highest thermic effect (remember this from ‘Nutritive Alternatives’?). A higher intake stabilizes blood sugar.

Chapter IX: The Closing Thought

We have mastered the process of consuming protein our ancestors couldn’t dream of. We stripped away all the noise and created tools like whey isolate and EAA supplements for maximum efficiency. But amidst all the timing for MPS, PDCAAS scores, and heavy metal warnings, it’s easy to lose sight of the bigger picture.

Protein isn’t just a macro on a spreadsheet. It’s the fundamental piece for our wellbeing. When you eat a rotisserie chicken, hummus, or a scoop of protein powder, you’re dismantling the work of another living thing to rebuild yourself. You are participating in the continuous, 4 billion year old cycle.

Don’t stress over the perfect 30-minute window for MPS or the 0.6 µg of lead. Ultimately, protein is just fuel for the machine. Respect the design, eat enough to satisfy the requirements, and keep building.
